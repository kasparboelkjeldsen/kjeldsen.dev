01 / THE BIG IDEA

One protocol. More possibilities.

Why a shared language between AI and your tools is such a powerful idea.

There is a familiar moment in almost every useful AI conversation: the idea is clear, but the work is somewhere else. The content lives in a CMS. The image sits in a folder. The structure exists behind an API. Getting between those places can take more effort than the idea itself.

A connection worth getting excited about

Model Context Protocol gives AI applications a common way to connect to servers that expose capabilities. Tools describe actions an application can call; resources supply context; prompts provide reusable interaction templates. That shared vocabulary makes it possible to discover what a server offers instead of treating every integration as an entirely separate conversation. The official MCP architecture overview explains these building blocks.

To me, the exciting part is what that makes possible in everyday work. Imagine asking for a blog and having the next step be an inspection of the actual CMS: its existing types, its editors, its content tree. The conversation now has something concrete to work with.

Less translation, more making

This journal is a small example. Through the local Umbraco MCP server, we created page types, configured a block grid, imported supplied photographs, and published content. Those are separate CMS operations, but they belong to one understandable task: make a place for two stories.

My favourite benefit is continuity. A heading can travel from an idea into an editable content field, while the page structure stays recognisable to an Umbraco editor. The result is something a person can inspect and revise in the backoffice.

The useful kind of awesome

A protocol does not make every decision correct. Good naming, clear content models, and checking the saved result still matter. In fact, a connection to real tools makes those habits more valuable: there is now a real result to check.

That is why MCP feels promising. It brings the conversation closer to the work. Fewer disconnected handoffs, more tangible progress, and more room to spend attention on the thing you wanted to make in the first place.
